[Post 15](../015-teacher/)'s planner landed all 200 gauntlet courses, but its copies of the
simulator knew things no pilot can: when each frog would strike, the wind, the gusts to come,
the drone's mass. Its best student, a network, landed 22. This post asks two questions:
1. **Can a planner land all 200 knowing only what a pilot could?**
2. **Can a network learn to fly from it?**

The courses, threats and 200 test courses are [post 14](../014-gauntlet/)'s.

**New here? The words in this post, in plain terms:**
- **Course (the gauntlet).** A simulated flight of 60–120 m over water, from a start pillar to
  a landing pad, past birds and missiles that chase the drone and "frogs" that leap at it.
- **Pilot.** Whatever decides, 20 times a second, how hard the drone's motors push and how far
  it leans.
- **Planner.** A pilot that thinks ahead by trial: every 0.2 s it imagines about a thousand ways
  the next few seconds could go, each in a copy of the simulator, and picks the one that goes best. It works, but
  it's slow.
- **Fair.** The planner may only use what a real pilot could know: what it has seen, and what it
  can feel from its own motion.
- **Network.** A small neural network: a fast formula with many tuned numbers that maps what the
  drone senses to a decision, learned from examples instead of written by hand.
- **Training (DAgger).** The network flies, the planner says what it would have done at each
  moment, and the network is adjusted to agree, round after round.
- **Shield.** The two together: the network suggests its 5 best moves, and the planner checks
  only those, which is far less work than checking everything.
- **CPU and GPU.** A laptop's processor does a few things at a time; a graphics card (GPU) does
  thousands of small identical things at once, which suits imagining many flights in parallel.

**Short answer:**
- **The planner can, with a nimbler drone.** Knowing only what it has seen, it lands 168 of 200
  with post 14's 30° tilt limit. With 45° and three fixes it lands **200 of 200**, and 200 of
  200 on new courses.
- **A network alone can't.** Trained on the planner's choices, it lands **1 to 6** of 200 after
  every round.
- **A network plus a short check can.** The *shield* searches only the network's 5 favourite
  moves and falls back on a full search when all of them fail. It lands **200 of 200** on the
  test courses and **200 of 200** on new ones. The full search ran in 2% of decisions.
- **A rented GPU made it fast.** The planner flies the 200 courses in 85 s instead of 40
  minutes; a full training run takes 14 minutes instead of 4½ hours.

## A planner that knows only what a pilot knows

Post 15's planner copies the simulator every 0.2 s, flies each copy ahead with a different
plan (a cruise, a sharp dodge, a landing run, a wait beside the pad), and follows the best.
Two things in those copies were unfair, so I took them away:
- **Frogs.** They strike on a 3–4 s beat, each strike up to 0.1 s off it. The copies now know
  only the strikes the drone has seen (within 40 m), which bound the next one to an interval.
  Each plan is flown under three timings in that interval and scores by its worst.
- **The air and the mass.** The copies now use estimates. Each update is replayed in still air;
  how far the real drone's motion differs gives its mass and the air's push.

| Planner, 200 test courses | Landed |
|---|---|
| Fair, 30° tilt limit (post 14's drone) | 168 |
| Fair, 45° | 180 |
| + a frog owns its column while a strike could be on its way; waits past the pad | 193 |
| + margins round every threat in the copies; higher waits by the pad | 199 |
| + the tie-break toward a plain cruise heads to the pad | **200** |
| Same planner, 200 new courses | **200** |

**Agility mattered at the pad.** The pad's frog leaves a quiet window of about a second after
each strike, and the planner knows the strike's timing only to ±0.35 s. From a safe spot beside
the pad, a drone limited to 30° needs about 1.5 s to dash in and stop; at 45°, about 1.05 s. By
the end the extra tilt was worth less: the final planner at 30° lands 198.

I tuned these fixes on the test courses' failures, so the new courses are the real test.

## Can a network learn it?

Post 15 taught a network the planner's *sticks*. Averaging discrete choices (dodge up or down,
wait or go) broke even plain flying. This time the network picks one of the planner's **59
moves**, and post 15's controller flies it. It also gets what the planner believes: the air's
push, its mass, and when each frog may strike next. Training is DAgger, as in post 15: the
planner flies 400 courses, then the network flies more and more of each round's courses while
the planner labels every state.

| Round | Network alone, CPU run | Network alone, GPU run |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (planner's flights) | 4 | 1 |
| 1 | 4 | 4 |
| 2 | 6 | 1 |
| 3 | 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 4 | 6 |
| 5 | 3 | 3 |

It picks the planner's move in 90–94% of held-out states, but a flight is hundreds of decisions
and the gauntlet forgives few. As the network took over the training flights, fewer landed:
128 of 250 in round 1, 4 in round 5.

**The shield.** Every 0.2 s, the planner searches only the plans that start with one of the
network's 5 top moves. If every one of them fails in the copies, it runs the full search.

| Shield (the GPU run's final network) | Landed | Full searches |
|---|---|---|
| 200 test courses | **200** | 310 of 16,029 decisions |
| 200 new courses | **200** | 267 of 15,755 |
| 200 test courses, top 3 moves only | 199 | 387 of 16,383 |

So the network learned a shortlist, not a choice: a safe move is among its top 5 about 98% of
the time. On my laptop the shield takes about half the planner's time (1,300 s against 2,370 s
for 200 courses). Cutting the shortlist to 3 moves lost one course and saved only 6% of the
time (1,224 s).

## CPU and GPU

The same training ran twice: on my laptop's 10 CPU cores (250 flights a round) and, ported to
PyTorch, on a rented H200 GPU (300 a round). The GPU copy makes the same decisions as the
numpy code: checked decision by decision, every chosen plan matched. It runs all drones' copies
in one batch, in single precision. Both runs reach the same result, 1 to 6 landed alone. The
GPU's took **14 minutes** against **4 h 26 min**, about $1 of rental.

## What's next

The shield still leans on the planner's search, at half the planner's cost, and a shorter
shortlist barely helps. Next: find where the shield's time goes, train a network that is right
more often (more data, the dangerous moments weighted more), and run the shield on a cheaper
GPU.

### What the planner believes about frogs

From the strikes it has seen, the next strike falls in an interval. One strike seen: the beat
could be anything in the course's range (3–4 s), so the interval is about 1.5 s wide. Two or more:
the beat is measured, up to the 0.1 s jitter and the 0.05 s it takes to see a strike start.
None seen yet: it may strike any moment, within a beat of the frog coming into sight.

Flying each plan under three timings (the interval's start, middle and end) wasn't enough: a
strike can come at any moment in between. So the copies also get a guarantee: from the
earliest possible start of each of the next three strikes until a leap started at the latest
would have landed, the frog owns its column, 2.8 m either side. A copy found there then counts as caught. Where the
frog holds and how it goes back is still judged under the three timings.

At 30° the fair planner's 32 failures were 15 timeouts, 12 frog hits, 3 birds and 2 drones
lost off the world's edge. All 15 timeouts ended 12.5 m past the pad, at the world's edge:
drones that overshot it and never came back (the tie-break below). Of the 12 frog hits, 4 were
at the pad and 8 at the mid-route frog. Hence the 45° drone (its dash under the pad's frog fits
the quiet window), and waits on the far side of the pad too, so a drone that overshot or
arrived during a strike can still set up a landing.

### The air and the mass, from the drone's own motion

Each update, the planner keeps the drone's state and sticks, and at the next update replays
them in still air with a mass of 1. How far the real climb rate differs gives the mass at once
(to about 0.004). How far the sideways speed differs gives the air's push, about 0.03 m/s²
accurate after a few seconds. The push is fitted as a steady wind plus a sine (the gusts) over
the last 10 s, trying every period from 2 to 5 s. The copies and the controller then fly in
that estimated air.

### Margins, because the copies' air is only an estimate

A plan that only just misses a bird in a copy may not miss it for real, because the copy flies
in estimated air. So the copies count a pass within 0.25 m of a bird's hit radius as a hit,
0.4 m for missiles and 0.3 m for frogs, and land only within 90% of the landing limits (80%
made the tall pads unlandable). The waits by the pad moved up to 1.2 and 2.5 m above it, so a
frog leaping at a waiting drone holds above the way in, not across it. The landing controller
now tilts at most 80% of the 20° touchdown limit; post 15's cap, 0.6 of the tilt limit, was 27°
at 45°.

### The tie-break that sent drones off the edge

Post 15 made its planner easier to copy: among plans scoring within 16 points of the best, it
takes the one whose sticks are closest to a plain cruise along the route. Past the pad (an
overshoot), "along the route" points away from it, and drones hovered at the world's edge until
time ran out. Turning the tie-break off landed 196, with drones stalling mid-route on near-ties.
Pointing the plain cruise at the pad landed all 200.

### The network

Its inputs are post 14's 318 (the drone, its route, up to 4 pursuers with their closest
approach, 3 s of each frog's motion) plus 32: the air's estimated push now, in 0.5 s and in 1 s,
the wind, gust, period and mass, for each frog whether it was seen, where it is, the next two
strike windows and how many strikes it has seen, and the move made last. Three layers (512,
512, 256) and two heads: which of the 59 moves the planner would pick, and for each move whether
every plan starting with it fails in the planner's copies (a danger sense, so far unused).

DAgger: round 0, the planner flies 400 training courses and labels every update; then five
rounds in which the network flies new courses, taking each update's move from the planner with
probability 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 and so on, the planner labelling every state. After each round the
network is refit on everything (8,000 steps) and flies the 200 test courses alone.

### The GPU port

90% of the planner's time went into one function: stepping a copy of the simulator, about 2 µs
per copy per update on a laptop core. A decision for 250 drones is hundreds of thousands of
copies, over a million near a frog, flown 90 updates ahead, then deeper searches from the most promising ones.

On the GPU, each copy is one row of a tensor, and every branch in the simulator (on the pillar
or flying, a frog's strike due, a touch on a pad) is a mask rather than a list of indices.
torch.compile fuses the update into a handful of kernels. The deeper searches (dodges for
copies a bird or missile still chases at the end, wait-then-land for copies near the pad) and
the final choice became segment operations over (drone, frog timing) groups instead of Python
loops.

Before trusting it:
- **one update**, in double precision against numpy: 12 random courses × 320 copies × 90
  updates, every number within 6×10⁻¹⁴ and every outcome identical;
- **whole decisions**, from the same states: 315 decisions (the shield's among them), every
  chosen plan identical, every candidate's score within 1.5×10⁻¹³;
- **flights**: the GPU planner lands 200 of 200 test courses and 200 of 200 new ones.

On the H200, an update for a million copies takes 1.26 ms (1.2 ns per copy). The training
run's first round, the planner flying 400 courses, took 180 s on the GPU and 6,504 s on the
laptop. The rental cost about $4.60 for the whole session, most of it building and testing. A
full training run is about $1 at the H200's $3.89/hour and should be under that on a cheaper H100.
