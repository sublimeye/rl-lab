[Post 10](../010-drone/)'s drone hopped between two pillars in empty air, knowing exactly where
everything was. This time I made its world more like a real one:
- the landing pad slides;

- the battery runs down;

One pilot still gets exact positions. The other has to find its way with a small depth camera.
I also tried CAPS, a way of training smooth control that post 10 was missing, and rebuilt the
simulator so it steps hundreds of drones at once.

**Short answer:**

- **Many drones in one step makes the simulator 7–17 times faster.** Training gets less of
  that: post 10's 5-million-step run takes 1.8 minutes instead of 4.3, because PPO's own
  learning step is now most of the time.
- **The new world is harder, but learnable.** The exact-position pilots land on 165–180 of 200
  test courses, and the camera pilots on 147–152. On courses without birds, both land 50–55 of
  56.
- **CAPS makes the sticks 6–9 times calmer, at a price.** In wind and with a payload it lands
  16–43 fewer of the 200 courses, seed for seed.
- **They never learned to dodge birds.** Blindfolding a pilot to the birds barely changes how
  often it hits one. A bigger penalty, a cost for flying close to birds, and training with three
  birds every flight didn't change that either. Most hits come with 0.6 seconds' warning or
  less.
- **The three-reading memory made no difference.** The camera pilot without it lands as often.

## Faster: many drones per step

In post 10, each drone was its own Python object, stepped one at a time. Now every number about
every drone lives in arrays: all the x positions in one, all the speeds in another. One numpy
call moves them all. With the new features switched off it's the same simulator, number for
number: a test flies 40 courses both ways with the same stick inputs and checks that every
position and speed matches exactly.

| Steps per second (one stick update for one drone) | |
| --- | --- |
| Post 10's simulator, one drone at a time | about 47,000 |
| Vectorised, 256 drones at once | 323,000 |
| Vectorised, 1,024 at once | 583,000 |
| Vectorised, 4,096 at once | 782,000 |
| Vectorised with this post's world, 1,024 at once: exact positions / camera | 276,000 / 136,000 |

That's 7–17 times faster to step. Training post 10's pilot again, with the same PPO settings
and the same 5 million steps:

| Post 10's task, 5 million steps | Minutes | Steps per second |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One drone at a time (16 processes, as in post 10) | 4.31 | 19,300 |
| Vectorised, 256 drones, 1 thread for the network | 2.00 | 41,600 |
| Vectorised, 256 drones, 4 threads | 1.77 | 47,100 |

So training is about 2.4 times faster: 0.17 minutes to reach 198 of 200 landed, against 0.42
minutes to reach 196. It isn't 17 times, because the simulator is no longer the slow part. In a
profile of the vectorised run, PPO's own learning step took about six times as long as stepping
the drones. One more try: settings
tuned for a fast simulator (1,024 drones, bigger batches, half as many passes over the data)
ran at 146,600 steps a second. But it learned less per step, and needed 11 million steps (1.3
minutes) to land all 200. That's slower to a good pilot, not faster.

The next big step would be the JAX route from my notes: the learning step and the simulator
both on a GPU. I've left that alone for now. The longest run in this post was 48 minutes.

## The new world

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Moving pad | On most courses (133 of the 200 test courses) the landing pad slides left and right, up to 2.5 m each way, one swing every 4–9 s. Landing speed limits count relative to the deck. |
| Birds | 0–3 per course, at 2–11 m, slow (1–3 m/s) or fast (4–7 m/s), bobbing a little. They fly straight across and come back in from the other side; touching one is a crash. They fly through the pillars; I let them. |
| Battery | Starts anywhere from 6% to 100%. Hovering for 20 s uses about 6%, full throttle 17%. Full thrust drops by about 1.5% at half charge, 3% at 20%, 12% at 5%, and the motors stop at 0%. Most of the time you don't feel it. |
| Sensors | Every reading has some noise (its size changes from flight to flight) and arrives 50 ms late. |
| Memory | The pilot sees its last three readings, not just the latest. |
| Weather | As post 10 learned: random wind, gusts and a payload of up to 35% on every training course. |

The **exact-position pilot** gets 26 numbers per reading:
- post 10's 14;
- the pad's speed and the battery;
- where the two nearest birds are and how fast they're flying.

The **camera pilot** gets 107:
- **11 from its own sensors:** speed, tilt, spin, motors, battery, altitude, its sticks, and a
  GPS-style fix on a beacon on the pad that's off by about 30 cm.
- **A 1D depth camera:** 32 rays fixed to the drone's body, all round except a 60° cone
  straight up, out to 15 m. Each ray says how far away the first thing it hits is, and whether
  that's the marked pad or a bird.

It doesn't know where the pillars are, how big the pad is, or where the birds are, except
through those rays. Both pilots are networks with two layers of 128, trained with PPO on 256
drones at once for 20 million steps, three seeds each.

The exact-position pilot learned in about a million steps (160–170 landed). After that, more

flat at the start costs a few landings, about as much as the "pragmatic" battery was meant to.

## Calmer sticks with CAPS

Post 10's pilot flew bang-bang, sticks slammed to the ends, and my two fixes there backfired.
CAPS (Mysore and others, ICRA 2021) works differently. Instead of charging the pilot for moving
its sticks, which it got round by holding them at the ends, it adds two terms to the training
loss itself:
- **Temporal:** the action now should be close to the action a moment later.
- **Spatial:** the action for a reading should be close to the action for a slightly nudged
  reading.

Both act directly on the network's output, so there's nothing to game. I used a weight of 0.3
on each. A 10-million-step trial at 1.0 was too smooth to fly well: its sticks barely moved
(0.019 per update), and it landed 89 of 200.

"Stick movement" adds up how far both sticks move from one update to the next (a full sweep of
one stick is 2). With CAPS the sticks move about 6 times less for the exact-position pilots and
9 times less for the camera pilots, and almost never sit at an end. On the trace above it's the
difference between a scribble and a curve.

It isn't free. A smooth pilot reacts more slowly. In calm air (same birds and pads, no wind or
payload) the CAPS pilots lose fewer landings (exact positions: 166–171 against 182–183). With
the wind and payload they trained in they lose more, and the camera pilot most: 109–130 against
147–152, mostly by crashing. The CAPS pilots also kept getting slightly worse late in
training: the exact-position ones peaked around 7 million steps (162–171), then slipped. The
camera pilot with CAPS opens the game above, because it's the one you'd want flying a real
motor. The plain one is in the menu.

## Birds: through, not around

The surprise. To check whether a pilot dodges birds, I blindfolded it: I replaced its bird
inputs with "no bird" and flew the same 200 courses.

| Exact-position pilot | Bird hits, seeing | Bird hits, blindfolded | Landed, seeing / blindfolded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seed 1 | 13 | 17 | 180 / 176 |
| Seed 2 | 15 | 13 | 175 / 185 |
| Seed 3 | 17 | 16 | 165 / 166 |
| Bird hit costs 15 instead of 5 | 19 | 14 | 167 / 180 |
| Plus a cost for flying within 2 m of a bird | 12 | 14 | 177 / 179 |
| Trained with 3 birds on every course | 14 | 18 | 158 / 140 |

Seeing the birds makes no consistent difference. The pilots fly the same quick path either way
and hit whatever happens to be in it. The three fixes I tried didn't teach dodging either:
- **A bigger penalty** for a bird hit.
- **A warning cost:** a small charge for every moment spent within 2 m of a bird, the usual
  trick for obstacle avoidance.
- **Training with three birds** on every course.

The last one does seem to use its bird inputs. Blindfolded, it crashes much more, because "no
birds" never happened in its training. But it doesn't hit fewer of them.

Part of the reason is how little time there is. Of the 90 bird hits in that table, 60 came
within 0.6 seconds of the bird first getting within 3 m: a fast bird flies at up to 7 m/s, the
drone about as fast. But 23 gave a second or more of warning, so some were avoidable, and none of these
pilots learned to avoid them. My guess is that a bird hit is too rare and too sudden a signal
next to everything else in the reward. Learning it would probably need training built around
birds: easy slow ones first, more encounters, longer runs. That's a post of its own.

## Memory

I added the three-reading memory so the camera pilot could tell which way a bird is moving. A
camera pilot trained with only the latest reading lands 153 of 200, the same as the ones with
memory (147–152). That fits the bird result: the pilots don't use where birds are going, and
their own speed already comes from their motion sensors.

## What didn't work at first

- **A camera that couldn't look up.** My first camera covered the ground and the sides, out to
  10° above horizontal, so a climbing drone couldn't see a bird above it. I widened it to
  everything but a 60° cone straight up before the final runs. It didn't fix the birds (see
  above), but a drone that dodges would need it.
- **CAPS at full strength**, above: 89 landings of 200. 0.3 was the setting I kept.

## How the AI runs on this page

Plain JavaScript again, one drone at a time.

- **Size:** the exact-position pilot has 78 inputs (three readings of 26) and 26,882 numbers;
  the camera pilot 321 inputs and 57,986 numbers. Stored as 16-bit floats, the files are 72 KB
  and 155 KB. The scores in this post are measured with those 16-bit weights.
- **Speed:** a decision, including casting the 32 rays, takes about 37 microseconds for the
  exact-position pilot and 81 for the camera pilot (Node, M1 Pro), 20 times a second.
- **Same results as Python:** a test replays six flights from both networks on courses with
  birds, sliding pads and a nearly flat battery. With the noise switched off it checks the
  drone's state after every update and the pilot's inputs and outputs along the way. It also
  checks that the networks the page loads are the ones it tested.

## Towards a real drone

Most of what makes this post's world realistic is exactly what breaks simulation-trained
pilots on hardware: noise, delay, wind, payload and a sagging battery. So the next step to real
hardware is shorter than it looks. Early notes, with nothing bought:

- **Small drones take "virtual sticks".** The cheap route is a Tello-class drone, whose SDK
  accepts four virtual sticks over Wi-Fi with about 100 ms of delay. The pilot would run on a
  laptop.
- **The network fits on a drone's own chip.** A research drone like the Bitcraze Crazyflie
  could run it on board: these networks are under 60,000 numbers. Its distance-sensor deck
  (front, back, left, right, up) is a coarse version of the depth camera here.
- **What's missing is 3D.** The simulator would need two tilts, yaw and a third axis, plus the
  real drone's command rate and delay. A printed marker on the pad, and a foam "bird" on a
  pole, would stand in for the rest.

The full notes, with rough prices and a step-by-step path, are in the repository.

## What I take from it

- **Vectorise the simulator first.** It's cheap to do. After it, the learning step is the
  bottleneck, which is the point where a GPU starts to pay.
- **CAPS does what penalties couldn't.** Smoothness in the loss, not in the reward, gives calm
  sticks with nothing to game. It costs some agility in rough air.
- **Having the information isn't using it.** The exact-position pilot was told where every
  nearby bird was and how fast it was flying, and flew as if it didn't know. Blindfolding a
  network is a cheap way to find out what it actually uses.

## Cost

All on an M1 Pro, CPU only. Speed comparison: 4.3 and 1.8 minutes per 5-million-step run. Main
runs, 20 million steps each, several at once: 17 minutes (exact positions), 26 (camera), 34
(exact positions, CAPS), 48 (camera, CAPS); the bird and memory experiments 13–20 minutes each.
No run came near the 5-hour limit.

```sh
cd experiments/011-drone2
uv run bench.py                                                               # raw simulator speed
uv run train.py --sim basic --task v010 --envs 16 --n-steps 512 --steps 5000000 --threads 1 --name basic-s1
uv run train.py --sim fleet --task v010 --steps 5000000 --name fleet-t4      # post 10's task, vectorised
uv run train.py --task state --name state-s1                                  # exact positions
uv run train.py --task vision --name vision-s1                                # camera
uv run train.py --task vision --caps 0.3 0.3 --name vcaps-s1                  # camera + CAPS
uv run train.py --task state --near 0.5 --name snear-s1                       # bird warning cost
uv run evaluate.py --export export.json                                       # site data
uv run evaluate.py --parity state-s1 vcaps-s1                                 # the JS test fixture
uv run summarize.py                                                           # every number quoted here
```
