**Short answer:**

- **Post 3's runner was weak, and a learned chaser shows it fast.** A chaser network trained
  from scratch against it catches it in 100% of episodes after 250,000 steps, less than half a
  minute of training.
- **Self-play helped one seed out of three.** Training the runner and a chaser against each
  other produced one runner (seed 3) that circles both pillars, where the chaser can't cut
  across.
- **Randomness is part of the defence.** The same network picking moves with randomness
  escapes a chaser trained specifically against it 98% of the time at 80% speed and 68–78% at
  90%. Taking its top choice every time, it escapes 19% and 2%.
- **Two fancier methods made it worse.** Details below.

## Why it was easy to catch

I measured "how easy is it to catch" directly: freeze a runner, then train a fresh chaser
network against it with PPO, the same way the runner learned. I call that chaser an
**exploiter**. If an exploiter catches the runner every time, the runner has a hole a person
can find too.

Post 3's runner lasted 2.6–2.9 seconds on average against its exploiters, at both 80% and 90%
chaser speed, and escaped none of 200 episodes. The exploiter learned to get in front of it and
corner it, usually against a wall. The runner's only plan was "run laps along the walls", and nothing in
its training ever came at it from the front.

It isn't the physics. Both squares turn the same way: from full speed, stopping takes 0.25
seconds and reaching 90% speed in the other direction takes 0.65. The runner never learned
*when* to turn.

## Self-play

The fix I tried first: a second network learns to chase, and the two take turns training.

1. Start the runner from post 3's version; the chaser starts from scratch.
2. Train the chaser for a while against the current runner.
3. Train the runner against the current chaser, older chasers, and the post 3 scripted bots.
4. Repeat, 20 rounds, three seeds.

The chaser won that race. While it trained, it caught the runner in 98–100% of episodes in
every round, except the last two rounds of seed 3. Against post 3's scripted bots, the runners did improve: escapes from the
intercept bot went from 74–82% to 82–93%, and from the keyboard-like bot from 82–85% to
90–98%. But against the final learned chasers, two of the three runners escaped 0–4% of the
time.

Seed 3 was different. By round 20 it escaped 93–96% of the time against all three seeds' final
chasers.

## Can the runner win at all?

There's an old puzzle about this, the lion and the man in a round arena. A runner circling near
the wall can only stay away from a pursuer by roughly (1 − pursuer's speed ratio) × the circle's
radius. Here the biggest circle has a radius of about 3.7 units (half the arena's height, minus
half a square), and squares touch at 0.6 units apart. At 90% speed the gap is 0.37 units, so a
chaser on the inside track should win. At 80% it's 0.74, just enough. It's a rough argument (the
arena is a rectangle and the squares have momentum), but it matches what I saw.

The pillars change that, and seed 3 found out how.

## The test that matters

Every runner was tested the same way: a fresh exploiter trained against it for 2 million steps,
then 200 episodes. The numbers are escapes, with the average time before being caught in
brackets (20 seconds means never caught).

| Runner | Picks moves | Chaser at 80% | Chaser at 90% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Post 3 | top choice | 0% (2.8 s) | 0% (2.6 s) |
| Post 3 | with randomness | 0% (2.8 s) | 0% (2.6 s) |
| Self-play, seed 1 | with randomness | 0% (5.5 s) | 0% (3.9 s) |
| Self-play, seed 2 | with randomness | 1% (5.4 s) | 2% (4.7 s) |
| Self-play, seed 3 | top choice | 19% (11.6 s) | 2% (6.5 s) |
| **Self-play, seed 3** | **with randomness** | **98% (19.8 s)** | **68% (16.7 s)** |
| Same, second exploiter seed | with randomness | 98% (19.6 s) | 78% (17.6 s) |

Randomness only helps a runner that has a good plan. Post 3's runner and self-play seeds 1 and
2 get caught just as fast either way, because running into a corner randomly is still running
into a corner. For seed 3 it's the difference between 19% and 98%. My explanation: with
randomness, the exploiter can't learn one exact ambush. It doesn't know which way around the
pillars the runner will go next.

Sampled results move a few points from run to run, since the randomness changes every
episode.

## Two things that made it worse

**A league.** I took seed 3 and trained it against a pool of the strongest chasers so far,
including the exploiters, three seeds. In training it escaped 88–98% of the time, and one of
them escaped 98–100% against the two exploiters that had broken earlier runners. Then fresh
exploiters held it to 0–6% escapes (5.3–7.7 seconds). Many of the pool chasers had the same
weakness: they got stuck pushing against a pillar instead of going around it. The runner learned
to exploit that, and lost its general skill.

One league runner looked unbeatable: 100% escapes against its 80% exploiter. A second exploiter
with a different seed held it to 6% escapes by 1 million steps and 4% by 2 million. Exploiters can fail to find
a hole that exists, so a single test isn't proof.

**PSRO.** The textbook fix for that: each iteration, train a fresh exploiter against the current
runner, add it to the pool, then train the runner against the pool. Every chaser in the pool is
then one that really beat the runner. Over 8 iterations, from the second one on, every new
exploiter still caught the runner in 98–100% of its last training episodes. Worse, the runners forgot how to beat the
simple scripted bots: against the intercept bot, escapes fell from 93% to 14–50%. Fresh
exploiters caught all three in 0–2% escapes, even with randomness. I'd guess only 10% scripted
opponents was too few, and the pillar orbit got trained away. I haven't tested that.

## How the AI runs on this page

- **What's stored:** only the part of the network that picks moves. That's 9 inputs, two layers
  of 64 numbers, and 9 outputs: 5,385 numbers per network. Training saves a PyTorch file; an
  export script copies those numbers into a JSON file (`models.json`), rounded to 5 decimal
  places. This page's file holds 5 networks: 264 KB, 92 KB gzipped.
- **How it loads:** the page is static HTML from GitHub Pages. The game is a small script, 11.4
  KB before compression, which includes the physics and the network code. When it starts, it
  `fetch()`es `models.json`. On post 3's live page, the file arrived about 130 ms after the
  request, right after the page finished loading. Until then the Start button stays disabled.
- **How it runs:** 20 times a second, the game builds the same 9 numbers the network saw in
  training, multiplies them through the three layers in plain JavaScript, and picks an action.
  One decision takes about 8 microseconds. No TensorFlow.js, no ONNX, no server.
- **How I know it matches:** a test replays games recorded in Python and checks that the
  JavaScript physics and network give the same results, step for step. It runs on every push.

## What I take from it

- **Test against a learner, not a script.** The scripted bots said post 3's runner was fine.
  A chaser with 20 seconds of training said it wasn't.
- **Use more than one exploiter.** One of them failed to find a hole that a second one found.
- **Unpredictability is a real strategy.** Against an opponent that learns your habits, a
  slightly random plan beats a perfect but predictable one.
- **Self-play can stall.** Two of three seeds never got good, and the two methods meant to fix
  that both made things worse here. The best runner came from one lucky seed.
- **The arena decides a lot.** Without pillars to circle, at 90% speed, the theory says the
  chaser should win.

I haven't tested any of this against a person. Try the game and see if you can catch it.

## Cost

All on an M1 Pro, CPU only:

- Self-play, three seeds in parallel: 23 minutes.
- League: 18 minutes.
- PSRO: 27 minutes.
- Exploiter tests: 36 of them, about 3–4 minutes each, run several at a time.
- Scoring: about 3 minutes.

## Run it yourself

```sh
cd experiments/003-tag
for s in 1 2 3; do uv run selfplay.py --seed $s --rounds 20 & done; wait   # self-play
# a fresh chaser against one frozen runner (the "exploiter" test)
uv run exploit.py --evader sp-s3/evader_r20 --ratio 0.8 --sample --seed 11 --name ts80-sp3
uv run evaluate_selfplay.py        # scores every run with an exploit.json, writes the site data
uv run summarize_selfplay.py       # prints every number quoted here
```

The league and PSRO commands are in `selfplay.py --help` and `psro.py --help`.
