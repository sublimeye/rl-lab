Posts 1 to 7 were about moving: getting to a flag, running away, chasing. This time the agent
has to change its world. The tide comes in from the left, the house is on the right, and the
sea always ends up higher than the house's floor. If nothing is done, the house floods every
time.

The digger can walk, dig a grain of sand and drop it. Its reward says nothing about sand or
walls: it gets a little for every moment the house is dry and a penalty if it floods. Whatever
it builds, it had to work out for itself.

**Short answer:**

- **It learns to build a wall.** In round 1, after 2 million steps (3 to 5 minutes of
  training), it keeps the house dry on 185–198 of 200 maps it has never seen. Doing nothing
  floods all of them; random digging saves 9.
- **Its first wall leaned on the house.** In round 1, two of three training runs piled sand
  straight against the house's seaward wall, which in that world could hold a pile of any
  height. When I made sand spill over the roof instead, those two saved only 81 and 101 houses
  out of 200. The third run had built a free-standing dam far out and still saved 195.
- **Round 2 builds a real bank.** Trained with the roof rule, all three runs build a mound 2–5
  columns in front of the house and keep 197–199 houses out of 200 dry.
- **It builds high, not to the forecast.** It's told how high the tide will rise, but it mostly
  ignores that: its walls are about the same height whatever the tide. It beats a hand-written
  builder that builds to the forecast on 14–15 maps, and holds up much better than it on tides
  higher than any it trained on.

## The world

Everything is a grid of 40 columns by 20 rows, and both sand and water are just a height per
column.

| | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Sand | Grains stack. A column more than 2 cells higher than its neighbour sheds a grain onto it, so piles have sides no steeper than 2 cells per column. The bottom row is bedrock. |
| Water | Water flows from a column to a lower neighbour through "virtual pipes": a difference in level pushes water through, and the flow keeps some momentum, so the tide arrives as a wave. A column never gives away more than it holds, so dry sand above the water stops it completely. |
| Tide | The sea, off the left edge, rises for the first 70% of the episode (350 of 500 steps) and then stays at its peak: 1 to 3 cells above the house's floor. |
| Map | Random every episode: a flat beach, a few bumps and dips, a hill up to the house. The ground in front of the house is always below the peak tide. |
| House | The last 5 columns. It floods when water on its floor gets 0.05 cells deep. |
| Digger | Walks one column per step, climbs steps up to 2 cells, can wade. It digs a grain from the column to its left or right, carries up to 3, and drops them left or right. It can't dig, drop or walk on the house. |
| Reward | 1/500 for every step the house is dry (a dry episode adds up to 1), and −1 when it floods. |

Each step the network sees 124 numbers: the sand height and the water depth of every column,
where it stands, how many grains it carries, the sea level now, the peak it will reach, and the
time.

## Training

PPO from Stable-Baselines3, a network with two layers of 128, 16 copies of the world at once, 20
million steps. That's 33–46 minutes per run on a laptop CPU, three runs per round with different
random seeds. Every saved checkpoint plays the same 200 test maps, which training never uses.
It picks its moves at random in proportion to how much it likes each one, the way it did in
training (more on that below).

To know what a decent strategy scores, I wrote a **scripted builder**. It picks the spot that
needs the least sand to stand above the peak tide, digs a few columns away and piles sand there
until the top is above the peak. It keeps the house dry on 185 of the 200 maps, moving about 10
grains. It has almost nothing to spare: on the other 15 the water gets there first. On map 1
of the replays below, its wall tops out just under the high-tide line.

What the runs build isn't the same:

- **Seeds 2 and 3 pile sand against the house.** On every test map they keep dry, the top of
  the pile is in the column right next to it. In this world the house is a solid block, and
  sand can't slide onto it. Sand against it can stack as high as the digger likes, so the house
  props up one side of the wall and only the seaward side needs a slope. It's cheap, and it
  works.
- **Seed 1 builds a free-standing dam far out**, its top 14 or 15 columns from the house, and
  gets the sand by digging a pit behind it, on the house side; on the maps I watched, down to
  bedrock. The pit stays dry because the dam holds.

All three move far more sand than they need: 31–72 grains per episode, against the scripted
builder's 10.

## Round 2: a house with a roof

Real sand piled against a wall can only go as high as the wall; any more and it falls onto the
roof. So for round 2 the house's wall is 2 cells tall, and a grain that would stand above it,
against the house, spills over and is gone. Since the tide rises 1–3 cells above the floor,
a pile against the house now only works while the tide stays below the top of its wall.

The round 1 networks, dropped into this world without retraining:

| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed 1 | free-standing dam | 195 of 200 |
| Seed 2 | against the house | 81 of 200 |

## Does it read the forecast?

The network is told how high this tide will rise; the dashed line shows it. The scripted
builder uses that number: it builds to the first whole cell above it. To see whether the network
uses it too, I ran the final networks on tides lower and higher than any in training. Training
tides peak 1 to 3 cells above the house floor.

| Peak above the house floor | 0.3–1 | 1–3 (trained) | 3–4.5 | 4.5–6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Round 1 networks, houses dry | 198–200 | 195–200 | 177–198 | 80–176 |
| Round 2 networks, houses dry | 199–200 | 197–199 | 182–188 | 100–139 |
| Scripted builder, round 1 world | 200 | 185 | 119 | 64 |
| Scripted builder, round 2 world | 200 | 185 | 119 | 54 |
| Average peak above the floor | 0.65 | 1.99 | 3.74 | 5.24 |
| Round 2 seed 1's wall top above the floor | 5.04 | 5.41 | 5.67 | 5.68 |

Houses dry out of 200 test maps; the wall top is the highest column the digger built up,
averaged over the maps.

From the lowest tides to the highest the sea rises 4.6 cells more, but the top of round 2 seed
1's wall rises 0.6 cells. The other five networks are the same: their walls rise 0.25 to 1.2
cells. Mostly they build to a fixed height above the house, between 3.7 and 6.9 cells
depending on the run, with plenty of room to spare for the tides they trained on. The builder's
wall follows the forecast (1.6 cells above the floor at the lowest tides, 4.5 at the highest),
but on the trained tides its top is only 0.75 cells above the water on average.

So the network's habit of overbuilding, which looks wasteful on easy maps, is what saves it on
tides it has never seen.

## Its top choice isn't enough

The networks pick each move at random, in proportion to how much they like it. Take away the
randomness and always play the top choice, and they get worse, sometimes much worse:

| | Picks with randomness | Always its top choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Round 1, seeds 1, 2, 3 | 195, 200, 198 | 160, 189, 188 |
| Round 2, seeds 1, 2, 3 | 199, 197, 197 | 151, 134, 170 |

Houses dry out of 200, final checkpoints. The top-choice score also jumps around between
checkpoints: round 1 seed 1 falls to 14 of 200 at 4 million steps, while picking at random it
saves 170. In the top-choice episodes that flood, the digger usually ends up repeating a dig or
a drop that can't do anything any more (its hands are already full, or empty) until the water
arrives. Sometimes it loops instead, digging on one side and dropping on the other, over and
over. The randomness it was trained with breaks it out of both, so the game on this page plays
with randomness by default.

## How the AI runs on this page

Plain JavaScript, no ML library, no server.

- **Size:** 124 inputs, two layers of 128, 7 outputs: 33,415 numbers, 275 KB of JSON per
  network (95 KB gzipped). The page loads a network when you pick it.
- **Speed:** one step takes about 40 microseconds in Node on an M1 Pro, 35 of them for the
  network and under 3 for the sand and water.
- **Loading:** in three fresh loads of this page, the network file (99 KB over the wire) had
  arrived 0.28–0.55 seconds after navigation, so the game was ready in about half a second.
- **Same results as Python:** a test rebuilds random maps from their seeds and replays five
  exported episodes in the round 2 world: random moves, the scripted builder, doing nothing and
  a round 1 network on two maps, where it spills sand over the roof. It checks the sand, water,
  position, spilled grains, inputs and reward every 10 steps, and the network's outputs against
  PyTorch's, on every push.

## What didn't work at first

- **Water that crept.** My first water spread a fixed share of each level difference to its
  neighbours. It moved like syrup and stayed about 3 cells behind the tide, so the house never
  flooded: doing nothing kept all 200 houses I tried dry. Water that keeps momentum keeps up
  with the tide.
- **Walls too expensive to build.** With piles only 1 cell steep and 2 grains per trip, a wall
  4 cells high needs long slopes on both sides, and even my scripted builder saved only 19 of
  the 200 houses I tried. With 2-cell slopes and 3 grains per trip it saves 185 of the 200 test
  houses in the final world.

## What I take from it

- **It finds the cheapest thing that works, not the thing I had in mind.** I expected a dam.
  Two of three runs found that the house itself was the best wall. That was a flaw in my world,
  not in the agent, and it took a rule change to get the dam I'd pictured.
- **Different runs, different solutions.** Same world, same settings, different seeds: one
  built a dam out in front and two leaned on the house. Both kept the house dry, so the reward
  couldn't tell them apart. The roof rule could.
- **Overbuilding is a strategy.** It's told the forecast and mostly ignores it. With no reward
  for saving sand, building high every time costs it nothing, and it holds when the forecast is
  higher than anything it has seen.

## Cost

All on an M1 Pro, CPU only. Training: 20 million steps per run, 33–46 minutes each, with three
or four runs going at once.

## Run it yourself

```sh
cd experiments/008-flood
uv run train.py --seed 1 --name s1                         # round 1
uv run train.py --seed 1 --house-wall 2 --name wall-s1     # round 2
uv run evaluate.py s1 s2 s3 --export round1 --live s2 --model s1
uv run evaluate.py wall-s1 wall-s2 wall-s3 --export round2 --live wall-s1 --transfer s1 s2 s3
uv run evaluate.py s2 --parity                             # the JS test fixture
uv run summarize.py round1 && uv run summarize.py round2   # every number quoted here
```
