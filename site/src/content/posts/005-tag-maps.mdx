The runner from [post 4](../004-tag-selfplay/) learned one trick: run a big oval around the two
pillars. It only works in that room. On other maps it runs into walls and gets caught: against
the intercept bot it escaped 3% of the time.

So this time every episode is on a random map: pillars, blocks, walls, small mazes, or a mix.
The runner gets 16 distance sensors so it can see the walls around it. Press **New map** for
another one.

**Short answer:**

- **Seeing the walls matters.** Trained only
  on random maps against scripted bots, a runner with 16 distance sensors escapes the intercept
  bot on 60–80% of maps it has never seen. Without the sensors: 52%. Post 4's runner: 3%.
- **Bots alone still leave it easy to catch.** A chaser network trained against that runner
  catches it 98% of the time after 2 million steps.
- **Self-play helped all three seeds this time**, not one lucky one. After 40 rounds, a fresh
  chaser trained against the runner for 5 million steps still lets it escape 23–46% of the time
  at 90% speed. After 20 rounds: 8–13%.
- **The model barely grew:** 6,409 numbers instead of 5,385. A network 3.3 times bigger did no
  better.

## The maps

Five kinds of maps, all in the same 12 × 8 room:

<MapGallery
  seeds={[1, 2]}

The rule that keeps them playable: every obstacle is an island. A pillar, a block, or a
straight or L-shaped wall is placed only if it stays at least 1.2 units (two squares) away from
the outer walls and from every other obstacle. So a square can always go around anything, there
are no dead ends, and no part of the room is closed off. After placing them, a flood fill
checks it again: it walks every spot where a square fits and fails the map if any spot can't be
reached. In 7,500 generated maps it never failed. A test with a closed box confirms the check
does catch one.

A map comes from a seed. The browser and the training code run the same small random number
generator, so map #42 is the same map in both, and a test checks that.

## Seeing the walls

In posts 3 and 4 the runner got 9 numbers: where it is, where the chaser is, and how both are
moving. That's enough when the room never changes. Now it also gets 16 rays, one every 22.5°,
each saying how far the nearest wall or obstacle is in that direction (up to 6 units).

The scripted bots needed an upgrade too. In post 3 they steered around round pillars. Now they
plan a path on a grid of half-unit cells and follow it, so they can get around walls and
through mazes. Against a square that stands still or just runs away, they catch it on all
1,000 test maps I tried.

## Step 1: train against the bots

Same recipe as post 3: PPO against a mix of scripted bots, 10 million steps, but on a new map
every 4 episodes. Every number below is from 200 test maps (40 of each kind) that no network
trained on, with the chaser at 90% speed.

| Runner | Pursuit | Intercept | Keyboard | Old room |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Post 4's runner (one room, no sensors) | 14% | 3% | 6% | 94% |
| No sensors, trained on random maps | 86% | 52% | 70% | 73% |
| 16 sensors, three seeds | 86–92% | 60–80% | 70–82% | 78–88% |
| 16 sensors, network 3.3× bigger | 86% | 63% | 68% | 78% |

Escapes out of 200 episodes against each scripted bot, the runner picking moves with some
randomness (as in the game). Keyboard is the bot that moves like a person on arrow keys. Old
room is post 3's room against the intercept bot, with its own 200 starts.

Three things stand out:

- **Post 4's runner only knew its room.** It escapes the intercept bot 94% of the time in post
  3's room and 3% of the time anywhere else.
- **The sensors help most where it matters.** Against the simple pursuit bot, the runner
  without sensors does as well as the others. Against the bots that aim ahead it falls
  behind: 52% against the intercept bot, compared with 60–80%. My guess: it can't see a wall
  coming, so it can't plan a loop around one.
- **A bigger network didn't help.** Two hidden layers of 128 instead of 64 (21,001 numbers
  instead of 6,409) scored the same as the same seed at the normal size, and took a bit longer
  to train. The sensors made the difference, not the size.

The runners also do fine in post 3's room, which none of them ever saw: 78–88% against the
intercept bot, where post 4's runner, trained in that room, gets 94%.

## Step 2: a learned chaser finds the holes

Post 4 showed that scripted bots are a weak test. So I froze the best step 1 runner (seed 3)
and trained a fresh chaser network against it on random maps, the same "exploiter" test as
last time.

It didn't last. At 90% speed the exploiter held it to 4% escapes after 500,000 steps and 2%
after 2 million, catching it in 3.9 seconds on average. At 80% speed: 6%. Same story as post 3's
runner: fine against scripts, easy for anything that learns.

## Step 3: self-play on random maps

Then the same self-play as post 4: a chaser network and the runner take turns training, 20
rounds, three seeds, every round on random maps. Each seed started from its own step 1 runner.

Two things were different from post 4. First, the chaser never ran away with it: in the last
round it caught the runner 72–80% of the time while training, where in post 4 it was 98–100%.
Random maps seem to make the chaser's job harder too. Second, all three seeds improved,
not just one. Against the final chasers of all three seeds, the runners escaped 4–15% of the
time at the start and 20–32% after 20 rounds. Against the scripted bots they stayed about
the same, within 9 points either way.

Then I ran 20 more rounds on top, to see if it was still improving. It was.

## How long until it breaks

The real test is the exploiter again: a fresh chaser trained against each final runner on random
maps, then scored on the 200 test maps, at 90% speed unless noted.

| Runner | Exploiter, 2M steps | Exploiter, 5M steps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step 1 (bots only), seed 3 | 2% (3.9 s) | |
| Self-play, 20 rounds, three seeds | 22–52% | 8–13% (7.7–8.6 s) |
| Self-play, 20 rounds, chaser at 80% | 34–64% | 19–36% |
| **Self-play, 40 rounds, three seeds** | **42–54%** | **23–46% (11.2–13.0 s)** |

Escapes, with the average time before being caught in brackets (20 seconds means never). The
2M column for 20 rounds covers two exploiters per runner with different seeds.

Three things I didn't expect:

- **The exploiters needed far longer.** Against the step 1 runner, 500,000 steps were enough.
  Against the self-play runners they hadn't caught it once at 250,000 steps, and most kept
  improving all the way to 5 million. Post 3's runner lasted about 250,000.
- **The 20-round runners weren't done.** With 20 more rounds, the best one (seed 1) went from
  13% to 46% against a 5M-step exploiter, and the exploiter stopped improving after 2 million
  steps.
- **80% speed wasn't always easier than 90%.** Two exploiters with different seeds on the same
  runner differed by up to 28 points, so read the ranges, not single numbers.

The maze replay flatters the runner a little. It tucks into the corner of an L-shaped wall,
and the chaser loses it: it goes back and forth on the other side of a wall until time runs
out. Some of the escapes are the chaser getting stuck, not the runner outsmarting it. On the
pillar map there's nothing to hide behind, and it's caught after 7.7 seconds.

The kind of map matters most. Against its 5M-step exploiter, the 40-round runner escapes on
68% of maze maps, 65% of wall maps and 48% of mixed ones, but only 40% of block maps and 12% of
pillar maps. Walls and mazes make long loops that the chaser can't cut across. A few round
pillars don't, and in open space a chaser at 90% speed on the inside track wins, as in post 4's
lion-and-man argument.

## How the AI runs on this page

Same as post 4: no ML library and no server. The network is a JSON file of weights, and a
small script runs it in plain JavaScript.

- **Size:** the runner reads 25 numbers now instead of 9: the 16 sensors are new. That adds
  16 × 64 weights to the first layer, so a network has 6,409 numbers instead of 5,385, 19%
  more. In the JSON file that's 61 KB instead of 51 KB per network, 21 KB instead of 18 KB
  gzipped. This page's file holds seven networks (five runners and two chasers): 407 KB, 147
  KB over the network.
- **Loading:** the game script grew from 11 KB to 18 KB (7.5 KB over the network) with the map
  generator and path planning. In three fresh loads of this page, the models request started
  273–356 ms after navigation and took 50–154 ms, so the game was ready in about half a
  second.
- **Speed:** one decision (sensors plus network) takes about 9 microseconds: about 1.2 for the
  16 rays and 7.9 for the network, measured in Node on an M1 Pro. The game decides 20 times a
  second, so that's under 0.02% of the time between steps.
- **Maps:** the map generator and its walk-through check run in the browser too, from a seed,
  so **New map** downloads nothing. A click takes about 33 ms in the browser, most of it setting
  up the scripted bots' path grid; the network runner doesn't need that.
- **Same results as Python:** a test rebuilds 30 maps from their seeds, compares sensors, bot
  paths, four episodes and the network's outputs with Python's, and runs on every push.

## What I take from it

- **Give it the senses the task needs.** Post 4's runner learned one room by heart. Trained on
  random maps with sensors, a runner also handled a room it never saw, and the one without
  sensors fell behind against the bots that aim ahead.
- **Don't trust a single exploiter run.** Two exploiters on the same runner disagreed by up to
  28 points, and most were still improving at 5 million steps.
- **Self-play needed more rounds than I gave it.** Twenty rounds looked close to done;
  forty were much better. I don't know yet where it stops.
- **The map decides a lot.** Loops make the runner hard to catch; open space with a few
  pillars doesn't.

I still haven't tested any of this against a person. Try the pillar maps if you want to win.

## Cost

All on an M1 Pro, CPU only:

- Step 1, five runs in parallel: 21–31 minutes each.
- Self-play: 20 rounds took 52 minutes, 20 more took 45 (three seeds in parallel).
- Exploiters: 17 runs, 6–8 minutes for 2 million steps and 13–15 minutes for 5 million, several
  at a time.
- Scoring on the test maps: a few minutes per pass.

## Run it yourself

```sh
cd experiments/003-tag
uv run train.py --maps random --rays 16 --steps 10000000 --seed 3 --name maps-s3
uv run selfplay.py --seed 3 --rounds 20 --evader-init maps-s3/ckpt_10000000 \
  --maps random --rays 16 --name msp-s3
uv run exploit.py --evader msp-s3/evader_r20 --ratio 0.9 --sample \
  --maps random --rays 16 --steps 5000000 --seed 12 --name mx90b-sp3
uv run evaluate_maps.py            # scores every run, writes the site data
uv run summarize_maps.py           # prints every number quoted here
```
