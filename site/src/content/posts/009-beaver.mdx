Post 8 was water against a defender, with the defender trained and the water just physics.
This time the water has a player too, and that player is you. It's a small board game: a spring,
a beaver's den, a few rocks and trees, and 24 rounds. Each round you dig one tile for the
water; then the beaver, a network trained with PPO, takes one step or drops one log. If water
reaches the den, you win.

**Short answer:**

- **It learns a real defence.** After 20 million steps of PPO, the beaver keeps its den dry in
  87–88% of games against scripted rivers on boards it has never seen. A hand-written beaver
  manages 41%, and doing nothing 0%.
- **It cuts the water near the source, and late.** Almost all its logs go onto water that's
  already flowing, a few tiles from the spring, around round 13 of 24. Each one drains about 4
  tiles of channel the river spent rounds digging.
- **You can beat it.** A river that plans ahead, trying each dig against the real beaver,
  floods its den on about 60% of boards. Rivers trained with PPO flood it on up to about a
  third.
- **Training against a learned river helped against learned rivers only.** Round 2 beat the
  river it trained with more often and was a little harder for a fresh one to exploit, but it
  did no better against the planner.

## The rules

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Board | 9 × 9 tiles, new every game: grass (soil), clay, low ground (old dry streambeds), rocks and trees, a spring and the beaver's den. The spring's own stream is already full when the game starts. |
| A round | You dig, then the beaver moves, then the water moves. 24 rounds. |
| Digging | One tile per round, and only next to the water: soil opens in one dig, clay in two. You can also pass. |
| Water | Spreads one tile per round into open low ground. It never gets more than 16 tiles from the spring, counted along open ground: that's its strength. Water cut off from the spring drains away at once. |
| Beaver | One step to a neighbouring tile, or one log onto a neighbouring low tile, wet or dry. A log blocks water on that tile for good. 4 logs per game. |
| No dams | Closer than 3 tiles to the spring (the current is too fast) and on the tiles touching the den (its way in). |
| Winning | Water in the den: the river wins. Dry after round 24: the beaver wins. |

So both sides are short of something. The river gets one dig per round, and the water moves only
one tile per round into what's dug, so opening two fronts takes twice as long as pushing one. The
beaver is short of logs and of legs: it can only plug a tile it's standing next to.

The no-dam tiles are there because of the first pilot. With no limits, it was learning to sit on
its den and plug each tile next to it as soon as it was dug: a den has at most four sides and
the beaver has four logs, so done properly that can't lose. That's no fun to play against. Banning dams on the
den's neighbours makes the beaver cut the water off further out. The ring round the spring is
for the same reason: three logs there and the river never gets going.

## Training

The beaver is a network with two layers of 128 trained with PPO. Moves it can't make are masked
out (MaskablePPO from sb3-contrib). It sees the board as 12 layers of 9 × 9 numbers: what each
tile is, where the water is, where the den and the beaver are, where the river dug last and
where its own last log went, the no-dam tiles, and, for every tile, the fewest digs the river
would need to get water there. It also sees how many logs and rounds are left. The reward is +1
if the den is dry at the end and −1 if it floods. Nothing for logs or for where they go.

It trains against **scripted rivers**. Each one plans the cheapest route from the water to the
den (fewest digs, then shortest) and digs the first tile on it that's next to the water. Each
training game draws a variant: some add random costs to tiles so the route changes from game
to game, some steer away from the beaver, and some dig a random tile now and then.

For tests there are 200 boards that training never sees, played against three fixed styles:
**route** (straight for the den), **dodge** (avoids digging near the beaver) and **wander**
(random detours, a random dig 30% of the time). That's 600 games per beaver. To know what a
sensible defence scores, I also wrote a **scripted beaver**. It predicts the scripted river's
route, dams low tiles on it when it can, and otherwise walks next to where the river
will dig next. It keeps the den dry in 245 of the 600 games (41%). Doing nothing keeps it dry in
none, and random moves in 22.

It gets there slowly but steadily: level with or past the scripted beaver by 2 million steps, then
on to **522–530 dry dens out of 600 (87–88%)** after 20 million steps. That's about two hours
per run on the laptop CPU, three runs side by side.

How it plays is nothing like the scripted beaver:

- **It cuts the water, not the dig.** 98–99% of its logs go onto water that's already flowing,
  and almost never onto the tile the river has just dug (1% or less). The scripted beaver plugs
  fresh digs in a fifth to a third of its dams.
- **It cuts near the source.** Its logs land 4.5–4.8 tiles from the spring, just past the
  no-dam ring, and about 6 tiles from its den. Each one drains about 4 tiles of water below it.
  The scripted beaver defends close to the den (about 3 tiles away) and drains next to nothing.
- **It waits.** Its logs go down, on average, in round 13 of 24; the scripted beaver's in round
  8\. Cutting late means the river has spent half its rounds on a channel that's now dry, and
  has to dig a way round the log with fewer rounds left.

## Can a river beat it?

The scripted rivers are simple: they head for the den and don't think about the beaver much.
You will. So I tried two harder rivers on the same 200 boards.

**A learned river.** Same network shape, trained with PPO for the other side: its actions are
the tiles it can dig, plus passing. I trained it for 10 million steps against an earlier beaver
(a 10-million-step run under the same rules), and it ended up winning 37% of its training
games. It wins by grinding: it digs in almost every round (about 22 digs a game) and floods
late, around round 19, after the beaver has used most of its logs (3.2–3.3 of 4). It's also narrow: against a beaver
that does nothing at all, it still leaves 16 of the 200 dens dry.

**A planning river.** No training. On each turn it tries every dig it could make. For each one it
plays the rest of the game out four times, with the real beaver network answering and the
scripted river digging after that first move, and it picks the dig that floods the den most
often. That's more than a person can do in their head, since it can run the beaver forward.
But it's a fair picture of someone who has played a few games and knows how the beaver tends
to react.

| Dens dry out of 200 | Scripted beaver | Round 1, seeds 1–3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Route river | 62 | 175–180 |
| Learned river | 113 | 129–134 |
| Planning river | 4 | 77–81 |

The planning river floods the trained beaver's den on about 60% of the boards, and the scripted
beaver's on 98%. Against it the beaver uses only 2.6–2.8 of its 4 logs. The planner usually
wins while the beaver still has logs to spare.

The beaver also needs its randomness here. It picks each move at random, in proportion to how
much it likes it, the way it trained. Always taking its top choice barely matters against the
scripted rivers, but against the planner it keeps only 45–53 dens dry instead of 77–81. A beaver
that always does the same thing in the same spot is easy to plan against.

## Round 2: training against the learned river

For round 2, three new beavers trained from scratch for 20 million steps. In each game the
river was drawn at random: 40% of games against the scripted mix, 60% against the learned
river, at one of three points in its training (2, 5 or 10 million steps).

## How the AI runs on this page

Plain JavaScript, no ML library, no server.

- **Size:** 974 inputs, two layers of 128, 9 outputs (82 for the river): 142,473 numbers for the
  beaver. Stored as 16-bit floats in base64, the file is 380 KB (about 285 KB gzipped). The
  scores in this post are measured with those 16-bit weights, so they're the browser
  network's scores.
- **Speed:** a beaver move (building its inputs plus the network) takes about 0.15
  milliseconds in Node on an M1 Pro; the rest of a round (digging, logs, water) takes a few
  microseconds. The pauses you see in the game are animation.
- **Same results as Python:** a test rebuilds 25 random boards from their seeds and replays
  eight exported games. It checks every tile and every wet tile after every round, the scripted
  river's and scripted beaver's moves, a checksum of the network's inputs, its outputs and
  moves, and a trained river's moves, on every push.

## What didn't work at first

- **A beaver that couldn't lose.** The first pilot, with no ban on dams by the den, learned to
  stand on its den and plug each neighbouring tile as soon as it was dug. That's the rule
  change described above.
- **A convolutional network.** Convolutions should suit a board game. On this laptop's CPU, a
  small one trained at about 750 steps a second, against about 6,700 for the plain network
  running alongside it, so I dropped it after a short test and went with the plain network.

## What I take from it

- **The cheapest defence is upstream.** Nobody told it where to put logs. It found that one log
  on the water near the spring, put down after the river has invested in a channel, undoes
  several rounds of digging at once.
- **Beating your sparring partner isn't the same as getting stronger.** Round 2 learned to beat
  the river it trained against and got no better against the one that plans. A learned
  opponent that grinds isn't a stand-in for one that thinks ahead.
- **For a game against people, randomness is a feature.** Its top-choice play is fine against
  bots that ignore it, and much worse against an opponent that predicts it.

## Cost

All on an M1 Pro, CPU only. Round 1: 20 million steps per beaver, about two hours each with
three or four runs going at once. Round 2: about two and a half hours each. The learned rivers:
78 minutes for 10 million steps, about 50 minutes for 5 million. The planning river takes a
couple of seconds per game, so it's only used for testing.

## Run it yourself

```sh
cd experiments/009-beaver
uv run train.py --side beaver --seed 1 --name b-s1                       # round 1
uv run train.py --side beaver --steps 10000000 --name mlp-a              # the earlier beaver
uv run train.py --side river --vs mlp-a/ckpt_10000000 --steps 10000000 --name x-mlp-a
uv run train.py --side beaver --seed 1 --name b2-s1 --vs bots bots \
  x-mlp-a/ckpt_02000000 x-mlp-a/ckpt_05000000 x-mlp-a/ckpt_10000000      # round 2
uv run evaluate.py --beavers b-s1 b-s2 b-s3 --best --lookahead --rivers learned=x-mlp-a --curves --export round1
uv run evaluate.py --parity b-s1 --parity-river x-mlp-a/ckpt_10000000    # the JS test fixture
uv run summarize.py                                                      # every number quoted here
```
