Post 6's seeker only saw you along clear lines of sight, but it saw in every direction at once.
Walk around a block and it had already looked behind you. That's not how people see, and it
was very hard to beat.

So this seeker has a front. It sees only inside a cone, 100° wide by default. It turns its head
at a limited speed, so looking behind takes a moment. It notices you only after you've been in
view for a short reaction time, and it's slower walking backwards than forwards. All four are
settings, and one network handles any mix of them.

You play the runner again. The lit wedge is what orange sees, and the notch on its square shows
where it faces. A **?** over it means it has glimpsed you but not noticed you yet; **!** means
it has.

**Short answer:**

- **It's much easier to escape.** With the default human-like senses, the new seeker catches
  post 5's runner on 34–44% of the 200 test maps. Post 6's seeker, seeing all around, caught
  69%.
- **It learned to keep looking around.** It turns its head almost as fast as it can, most of
  the time, sweeping its view across the map instead of looking where it walks.
- **Training with the cone helps most when you hide.** Post 6's seeker given the same cone
  catches post 5's runner about as often (38%), but it finds a runner hiding out of sight less
  often: 78% against 87–89%.
- **Field of view and reaction time matter most.** Narrow the view to 60° and it catches post
  5's runner 14–17% of the time; widen it to 200° and it's 49–55%. A head faster than 300°/s
  makes it worse, not better.

## Its senses

| Setting | Default | What it does | Trained on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Field of view | 100° | how wide the cone it sees in is | 60–200°, or all around |
| Head speed | 300°/s | how fast it turns to face another way; turning right round takes 0.6 s | 90–1,080°/s |
| Reaction time | 0.25 s | how long you have to be in view before it notices you | 0–0.4 s |
| Walking backwards | 50% | its top speed when moving straight away from where it faces | 40–100% |

The defaults are rough human numbers. Our eyes cover close to 200°, but we notice detail in a
much narrower cone; a quick head turn reaches a few hundred degrees a second; simple reactions
take about a quarter of a second. Moving sideways costs half as much as moving backwards (75%
speed with the defaults).

Post 6's seeker is the same thing set to see all around, turn instantly, react instantly and
walk backwards at full speed.

## Head and legs

Each step the network now makes two choices: one of 9 moves (8 directions or stop) and one of 9
looks (keep facing the same way, or turn toward one of 8 directions). The head turns toward the
chosen direction at the head speed, so turning far takes several steps.

The reaction time applies twice. You count as noticed only after you've been in the cone for
0.25 seconds, unless it saw you a moment ago. And a spot on its map of where you could be
counts as checked only after it's been in view that long, so a quick glance across a room
doesn't clear it.

Anything within three quarters of a body length always counts as in view, like hearing someone
right next to you. Without that, the scripted search bot couldn't even find a runner standing
at its feet.

## One network for any senses

Every training episode draws new senses: the field of view is all around 15% of the time and
otherwise 60–200°, the head speed 90–1,080°/s, the reaction time 0–0.4 s, and the backwards
speed 40–100%. The network gets them as inputs, so the sliders in the game work without
retraining.

On top of post 6's 49 inputs it gets 14 new ones: which way it faces, the four settings, and
for each of 8 directions, how much of its map of where you could be it could check by turning
that way (the parts it has a clear line of sight to). That last one is what makes turning the
head worth learning: it says where looking would pay off.

## Training

Same setup as post 6: PPO, random maps, the seeker at 85–100% of the runner's speed, and the
same mix of runners (post 5's runners 55%, the scripted hider 30%, a runner standing still out
of sight 15%). The runners still see everything, and they don't know where the seeker faces.

The network is the same shape as before, 64 by 64. I trained two seeds for 20 million steps,
twice post 6's length, because it was still improving at 10 million. For comparison I also
trained a wider network (128 by 128, one seed) and two seeds on the default senses only.

## Results

All with the default senses, at 90% of the runner's speed, on the same tests as post 6: post 5's
200 test maps against post 5's best runner and the scripted hider, and 200 starts with a runner
standing still out of sight.

| Seeker | Post 5's runner | Hider | Still runner | First sight of the still runner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Trained with any senses** | **34–44%** | **41–47%** | **87–89%** | **5.6–6.5 s** |
| Same, wider network (1 seed) | 40% | 38% | 94% | 4.9 s |
| Trained on the defaults only, seed 1 | 40% | 49% | 90% | 4.8 s |
| Trained on the defaults only, seed 2 | 26% | 30% | 64% | 9.2 s |
| Post 6's seeker, looking where it walks | 38% | 34% | 78% | 7.7 s |
| Search bot (scripted), same head rule | 18% | 20% | 96% | 5.8 s |
| *For reference: post 6's seeker seeing all around* | *69%* | *56%* | *94%* | *2.5 s* |

Catch rate within 20 seconds; ranges are over the two seeds. First sight counts 20 s when it
never sees the runner. Post 6's seeker and the search bot get a scripted head: it looks at the
runner while it sees it, otherwise where it's going (the search bot: at the spot it's heading
for).

What I read from it:

- **The cone takes away almost half the catches.** Post 6's seeker on the same tests: 69% with
  all-round sight, 38% with the cone. A runner it can't see is a runner it can't chase.
- **Training with the cone helps it search, not chase.** Against post 5's runner, which keeps
  moving, the new seeker is no better than post 6's (34–44% against 38%). Against runners that
  hide, it is: 41–47% against 34% for the hider, and it finds the still runner sooner (5.6–6.5
  s against 7.7 s).
- **It looks around.** A third of the time it's moving, it faces more than 45° away from where
  it's going; post 6's seeker with the scripted head, 13%. It turns its head at 247°/s on
  average, most of the 300°/s it's allowed.
- **The search bot isn't the better searcher anymore.** With all-round sight it found the still
  runner in 1.6 s; with the cone it takes 5.8 s, about as long as the trained seeker, and it
  still catches runners that move half as often.
- **Training only on the defaults isn't clearly better.** One seed did about as well as the
  seeker trained on any senses. The other got stuck at around 26% from 5 million steps on and
  never recovered. With two seeds I can't say more than that.

## What each sense does

The seeker trained with any senses, with one setting changed at a time from the defaults:

| Setting | Post 5's runner | Hider | Still runner | First sight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Defaults** | **34–44%** | **41–47%** | **87–89%** | **5.6–6.5 s** |
| View 60° | 14–17% | 33–40% | 80–86% | 7.9–8.6 s |
| View 140° | 40–52% | 41–42% | 89–91% | 5.1–5.5 s |
| View 200° | 49–55% | 36–43% | 90% | 4.4–4.9 s |
| View all around | 38–40% | 32–36% | 66–77% | 3.5–4.9 s |
| Head 90°/s | 34–36% | 42–44% | 86–90% | 5.8–7.0 s |
| Head 180°/s | 35–45% | 44–46% | 87–89% | 5.4–6.2 s |
| Head 600°/s | 26–41% | 36–46% | 87–88% | 6.1 s |
| Head 1,080°/s | 22–24% | 34–35% | 85–86% | 7.3–7.5 s |
| Reaction 0 s | 44–50% | 32–38% | 89–90% | 4.4–4.6 s |
| Reaction 0.1 s | 40–48% | 40–44% | 88–90% | 4.9–5.1 s |
| Reaction 0.4 s | 20–37% | 38–45% | 85–86% | 6.8–7.3 s |
| Backwards 40% | 32–45% | 38–47% | 84–88% | 6.1–6.6 s |
| Backwards 75% | 32–46% | 44–52% | 84–89% | 5.8–5.9 s |
| Backwards 100% | 34–52% | 44–60% | 85–88% | 5.6–6.4 s |
| Post 6's: all around, instant | 48–55% | 35–36% | 74–76% | 4.3–4.4 s |

- **Field of view matters most.** From 60° to 200°, catches against post 5's runner go from
  14–17% to 49–55%, and it finds the still runner in about half the time.
- **Reaction time is next.** No delay: 44–50%. Four tenths of a second: 20–37%.
- **A faster head doesn't help.** Between 90 and 300°/s it makes little difference. Beyond that
  it gets worse: at 1,080°/s it catches post 5's runner only 22–24% of the time. It turns its
  head at about 820°/s there, nearly flat out, and at that speed a 100° cone sweeps past a spot
  in about a tenth of a second, less than the reaction time, so what it sweeps past doesn't get
  noticed or checked. With no reaction delay, the same fast head catches 32–39%. It isn't the
  slower walking backwards: with that switched off too, the fast head catches 27–38%, against
  34–52% for the normal head.
- **Walking backwards slowly barely matters.** The differences are within the spread between
  seeds.
- **Against the hider there's no clear pattern.** The scripted hider hides from the seeker's
  line of sight, not its cone, and doesn't use where it faces.

One weak spot: with all-round sight this seeker catches the still runner less often than with
any cone. It finds it as often as with the defaults (86–88%) but catches it less (74–76%
against 87–89%). Watching those games, it gets within about a body length and then circles without
touching. All-round sight was only 15% of its training, and nothing between 200° and all
around ever came up, so my guess is it never learned that case properly. For all-round sight,
post 6's seeker is still the one to use.

## How the AI runs on this page

Same as before: plain JavaScript, no ML library, no server.

- **Size:** 63 inputs instead of 49, and 18 outputs (9 moves, 9 looks) instead of 9, so 9,426
  numbers instead of post 6's 7,945, 19% more. That's 91 KB of JSON per network. This page's
  file holds three seekers and post 5's runner: 321 KB, 113 KB gzipped.
- **Speed:** one decision takes about 95 microseconds in Node on an M1 Pro: 62 for the line of
  sight and the map, 21 for the inputs (the 8 "worth looking" numbers go over every cell), 11
  for the network. Post 6's seeker, timed the same way, takes about 70. At 20 decisions a
  second that's 0.2% of the time between steps.
- **Memory:** besides the network, each seeker keeps its own map of where you could be, when
  it last looked at each spot and how long each spot has been in view: about 7 KB on these
  384-cell maps.
- **Same results as Python:** a test replays six exported episodes (the trained seeker, the
  bots, the hider) and checks every position, the facing, what it has noticed, the map, every
  input and the network's outputs, on every push.

## What I take from it

- **What it sees matters more than how it was trained.** The same network goes from 14% to 55%
  against post 5's runner just by widening its view. The cone cost post 6's seeker almost half
  its catches, and training with the cone won back searching, not chasing.
- **Senses as inputs work.** One network handles any mix, and the sliders change its behaviour
  without retraining. The weak spot is at the edges: the case it saw least, all-round sight, is
  where it does worst.
- **A sense can be too good.** A head faster than its reaction time made it worse, because what
  it learned, sweep all the time, stops working when the sweep is too fast to notice anything.
  More training at those settings might fix it; I haven't tried.

The runners are the weak part of this test. Post 5's runner and the scripted hider don't know
where the seeker faces, so neither sneaks around behind it. You can. A runner trained against
this seeker, one that uses its blind spot, is the obvious next step.

## Cost

All on an M1 Pro, CPU only:

- Training: 5 runs in parallel, 146–151 minutes each for 20 million steps.
- Scoring every final seeker at 18 settings, the checkpoints, post 6's seeker and the bot: 8
  minutes.
