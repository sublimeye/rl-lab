[Post 16](../016-flawless/) left a planner that lands every course and a network that, copying
it, lands 1 to 6 of 200 alone. Posts 14, 15 and 16 each went straight to training. This one
looks first, and looks again each time it gets stuck. The courses, the 45° drone and the 59
moves are post 16's.

**New here? The words in this post, in plain terms:**
- **Course (the gauntlet).** A simulated flight of 60–120 m over water to a landing pad, past
  birds and missiles that chase the drone and "frogs" that leap at it.
- **Planner.** A pilot that thinks ahead by trial: every 0.2 s it imagines about a thousand
  ways the next few seconds could go and follows the best. It lands every course, slowly.
- **Network.** A fast formula with many tuned numbers, learned from examples.
- **Move.** What the network decides: one of 59, such as "cruise at 7 m up and 10 m/s" or a
  sharp dodge. A hand-written controller turns the move into motor commands.
- **Safe and fatal moves.** After a safe move the planner still finds a way through. After a
  fatal one, every plan it tries ends in a crash.
- **Practice and reward.** The network flies by itself, thousands of courses at once, and is
  nudged toward whatever scored well. The reward is that score.
- **Test, fresh and dev courses.** Courses nothing trains on: 200 test, 200 fresh, and dev
  courses for comparing versions, so the first two are flown once per pilot.

**Short answer:**
- **The copy had memorised its flights.** It picks the planner's move 98% of the time on
  flights it trained on and 63% on flights it hasn't seen. A pilot that ignores every threat
  did as well.
- **Same flights, a different question.** Fitted to "any move the planner found safe" instead
  of "the move the planner picked", it lands 48 of 200 test courses.
- **Practice took that to 162, and stalled.** Flying the same courses again showed why: the
  same ones failed every time, and it flew faster than the planner. Its reward paid for
  speed.
- **Paid only to land,** it gained about 7 points for the same practice and worked out the
  unguarded pad by itself.
- **Deciding 5 times a second instead of 20,** practice went about three times faster. After
  110 minutes on a laptop: **194 of 200 test courses, 190 of 200 fresh ones, and 193 of 200
  with no frog on the pad**, with no planner at flight time. Not yet all 200.

## First look: why the copy failed

From post 16's saved flights, training nothing: 410,000 moments, each with the planner's move
and its verdict on every other move.

**The accuracy was measured wrong.** Post 16's 90–94% held out single moments at random, and
each had its neighbours, 0.05 s away in the same flight, in the training set. With whole
flights held out:

| Post 16's recipe | Flights it trained on | Flights it hasn't seen |
|---|---|---|
| Picks the planner's move | 98% | 63% |
| Picks a fatal move | 0.9% | 13% |

- **It was no better than not looking.** A pilot that ignores every threat lands 4 of 200
  courses. Post 16's network lands 3.
- **The planner's pick is partly a coin toss.** It typically has 37 safe moves and takes one
  by a tie-break. Nudged by the size of the sensors' noise, it picks a different move 1 time
  in 10.

**So ask the same flights a different question.** The planner's verdict on every move was
already saved. Fitted to "put your probability on moves the planner found safe, any of them",
with the usual guards against memorising, the network lands **48 of 200 test courses**. No
new data.

## Practice, and where it stalled

The safe-move network then practises on its own flights (the method is PPO): 8,192 drones at
once on the laptop, taught only by post 14's reward. After 45 minutes it lands **162 of 200
test courses**. The same practice from nothing landed 0 of 1,000.

That was this post's first ending, and two things were wrong with it. 38 of 200 flights
still ended in a hit. And with no frog on the pad it landed none of 200: every course it had
seen had that frog. More practice was closing the gap by a point every five minutes, and
slowing.

## Second look: it was paid to hurry

I flew 1,000 unused courses 8 times each.
- **The same courses fail.** 450 courses landed all 8 times and 63 at most twice. A pilot
  with the same odds on every course would give 180 and 1.
- **It sprints.** A third of the time it holds the full-throttle dodge as its cruise. Its
  landings take 12.6 s; the planner's, on the same courses, 15.5 s.

The reward was post 14's: so much a metre, +20 for landing, −10 for failing, a small charge
per second, and a discount that makes a reward one second away worth 10% less. By my
arithmetic (an estimate) that makes a 7% chance of dying a fair price for one second saved.
It did what it was paid for.

So I changed the pay: nothing for speed, −50 for failing. Same start, same practice, dev
courses landed:

| Practice so far | Paid to hurry | Paid to land |
|---|---|---|
| 20 million decisions | 58% | 61% |
| 39 million | 63% | 70% |
| 59 million | 71% | 77% |

Its landings now take 22 s. And once a quarter of its courses had no frog on the pad, it
worked that out alone: none of 200 at first, when it hovered where the pad comes into sight
until its minute ran out, then 185 of 200.

## Third look: too many decisions

After 56 minutes this pilot landed 88% of the dev courses and gained a point every ten
minutes. So I watched one course it fails 8 times of 8.

A flock of three comes head-on: one bird level with the drone, two about 2 m below. The
network puts about half its probability on holding its height and an eighth on a cruise 6 m
lower. It draws a fresh move 20 times a second, so it does some of each, sinks 2 m in two
seconds, and meets the lower birds.

**With 20 decisions a second, no single decision matters.** Each one's share of the credit
is tiny, so practice learns slowly. Trying something else lasts 0.05 s, so a dive is never
really tried. And two good answers average into a bad one. The planner decides every 0.2 s.
Now the network does too, and holds its move in between. Same start, same pay:

| Practice | A move every 0.05 s | A move every 0.2 s |
|---|---|---|
| 20 minutes | 81% | 89% |
| 40 minutes | 85% | 96% |
| 56 minutes | 88% | |
| 110 minutes | | **98%** |

The pilot also stopped needing dice. Deciding every 0.05 s, drawing each move from its
probabilities beat always taking the top one. Deciding every 0.2 s, the top move wins, so
that is how the final pilot flies.

## The result

Each pilot flown once on each set of 200 courses:

| Pilot | Test | Fresh | No frog on the pad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Post 16's network (copies the planner's pick) | 3 | 5 | |
| Safe-move copy, before practice | 48 | 43 | |
| Practice, paid to hurry | 162 | 163 | 0 |
| **Practice, paid to land, a move every 0.2 s** | **194** | **190** | **193** |
| The fair planner (post 16) | 200 | 200 | |

That is 110 minutes of practice on a laptop, or 559 days of flying. I chose the pilot on
2,000 dev courses, where it lands 1,958.

One kind of encounter at every waypoint, 200 test courses each, hits per encounter:

| Encounter | Ignores every threat | Paid to hurry | This post's pilot |
|---|---|---|---|
| A bird | 175 of 407 | 4 of 664 | 1 of 667 |
| Two birds | 183 of 394 | 4 of 664 | 7 of 662 |
| The missile | 195 of 270 | 14 of 656 | 2 of 667 |
| A bird + the missile | 199 of 261 | 23 of 644 | 6 of 664 |
| A crossing flock | 122 of 492 | 50 of 617 | 6 of 662 |

## What it still can't do

- **It is still hit.** 16 of the 400 test and fresh flights fail: 6 caught by frogs, 6 hit by
  birds, 1 by the missile, and 3 that left the course or met the water. The planner lands all
  400.
- **More practice is a slow road from here.** Halving its failures on the dev courses from
  11% to 5% took 13 minutes. Halving them again, to about 2.5%, took 77.
- **It doesn't work the sticks.** A hand-written controller turns each move into motor
  commands, from the drone's true state. Post 14's network worked the sticks itself, on a 30°
  drone, and landed 33: a different pilot, not a like-for-like match.

Four more ideas didn't help: practising the courses it had failed, restarting practice just
before its own failures, rewinding failures to find the move that would have saved them, and
practising nearer to the way it is flown. They are in the details below.

## What I take from it

- **Look before training, every time it stalls.** Each look took minutes, trained nothing,
  and found a fault more training would not have fixed.
- **Hold out whole flights.** 94% of held-out moves right and 3 of 200 courses landed were
  both true of the same network.
- **Copy what a search knows, not what it picks.**
- **Practice chases the reward you wrote.** I asked for speed without noticing, and got a
  pilot faster than the planner that died one flight in five.
- **Decide as often as a decision matters, no oftener.**
- **Next:** the last 3 or 4 in 100, and a network that works the sticks itself, taught by
  this one.

<summary>About 6 more minutes, if you're interested: how each piece works</summary>

### How the copy fails, flight by flight

Without refitting anything: each round's network, on the next round's flights (which it never
trained on), picks the planner's move 59–67% of the time and a fatal one 11–13%, with no
improvement over five rounds, in both of post 16's runs. With a bird or missile about to pass
(closest approach within 2 s and 2 m) the fatal picks are 21–23%; with nothing in sight and no
frog near, 1–2%. In a failed flight the first fatal move comes about 1 s before a bird hit, 2 s
before a missile hit and 4 s before a frog's, and it is one of three or four. The flight is
beyond saving (no safe move left) only in its last 0.6–0.8 s.

It also repeats itself. When the planner wants a different move from the one the network made
last, the network keeps its own 76–81% of the time. Hiding that input alone didn't help
(fatal picks 14.1%). More flights help slowly: eight times as many take the fatal picks from
16.8% to 14.1%.

### What "fatal" means here

At a decision the planner flies about 900 plans ahead, grouped by the move each starts with.
A move's score is its best plan's, under the worst of three guesses for when a frog strikes.
Under −500, every plan starting with that move crashes within the look-ahead. It is the
planner's judgement, not ground truth. Used as a veto on post 16's network (skip any move its
second output expects to fail), it lifts 3 landings to 17.

### Nudging the planner

Forty flights on unused courses. At every third decision a copy of the planner, with the same
memory and the same last plan, decides again from a nudged world: the drone moved by 5 cm and
0.1 m/s, each bird and missile by 5 cm (the sizes of the sensor noise the network's inputs
carry). Of 1,050 decisions, 10% came out as a different move and 3% as a move the un-nudged
planner found fatal.

### The safe-move fit

For a moment where at least one move is safe, the loss is minus the log of the probability the
network puts on the safe moves together, plus a tenth of the usual loss on the planner's own
pick. Against memorising: dropout of 0.3, noise of 0.1 standard deviations on every input,
weight decay of 0.01, and the last-move inputs blanked. Same network as post 16's (512, 512 and
256 units). Either change alone lands 10–14 of 200 dev courses; together 42. I also took away
the planner's wind estimate, which replays every update on a copy of the simulator; that cost
nothing (40 of 200).

### What the pilot is, exactly

- **Inputs, 341:** post 14's 318 (the drone, the route, up to four pursuers with their closest
  approach, 3 s of each frog's motion), 16 for a memory of each frog's strikes (when the next
  two could start and end, worked out from the strikes seen within 40 m), and 7 for the move
  made last.
- **Output:** a probability for each of the 59 moves. Landing runs and waits are only on offer
  within 25 m of the pad, as in the planner. The final pilot takes the most probable move
  every 0.2 s.
- **Controller:** post 15's, with post 16's gentler touchdown, working 20 times a second. It
  reads the drone's true position and speed and assumes still air and the average mass.
- **Nothing else:** no search, no copies of the simulator, no planner object.

### The first practice, in numbers

PPO, 8 processes of 1,024 drones each. Each round is 48 decisions per drone, then 3 passes
over the 393,000 decisions in batches of 16,384. Learning rate 0.0001, clip 0.2, an entropy
bonus of 0.003; for the first rounds only the value network learns. Post 14's reward: 0.3 a
metre, +20 landed, −10 failed, 0.2 a second, discount 0.995 per decision of 0.05 s.

- **From nothing** (15 minutes): 0 or 1 of 400 dev courses at every check, 0 of 1,000 at the
  end. One run, one setting.
- **With a side task** (still fitting the saved flights, so practice couldn't forget them):
  749 of 1,000 dev courses after 45 minutes against 843 without; 145 test and 152 fresh
  against 162 and 163. Practice didn't need it.
- **Carrying a run on with a new value network** set it back (301 of 400 dev courses fell to
  255); the trainer now saves its value network.

### The second look, in numbers

The paid-to-hurry pilot, moves drawn, on 1,000 courses used for nothing else:
- 8 flights each: 80.7% landed. By encounter, its 983 bird and missile hits: bird + missile
  409, flock 226, two birds 156, the missile 127, one bird 65. Frogs caught it 376 times.
- 178 failed flights wound back and flown on 32 times each: alive 4 s past the original end
  when wound back 0.5 / 1 / 2 / 3 / 5 / 8 s: 23 / 37 / 51 / 56 / 59 / 60%. Three seconds before
  its end a failed flight is rarely beyond saving (7% have under a 10% chance), yet from 8 s
  back it still dies 40% of the time: the place is dangerous at that speed, not the last move.
- It is over 10 m/s, the move table's fastest cruise, 36% of the time. Taking the sprint off
  its menu at flight time doesn't help (774–783 of 1,000 against 822): it never practised
  without it.
- On the 63 courses it landed at most twice, five failures in six end the same way: the same
  threat on the same course. The planner lands 34 of the 35 it failed most, and 31 when told
  to assume still air and the average mass, as this pilot's controller does.

Paid to land: discount 0.9998 per 0.05 s, no charge for time (later 0.004 a move, to stop it
dawdling), −50 for a failure or for running out the 60 s. After 20 minutes it landed 810 of
1,000 dev courses (paid to hurry, at the same point: 76%). Carried on for 36 minutes with a
quarter of the courses unguarded: 878, and open pads 0, 0, 0, 140, 170, 183, 177, 185 of 200
at checks 20 million decisions apart.

### The third look, in numbers

- The paid-to-land pilot's failures fell from 30% at 39 million decisions to 12% at 208
  million: roughly one over the square root of the practice. On that line 1% needs about 100
  times more.
- Made to hold each drawn move longer with no retraining, it lands 880, 822 and 539 of 1,000
  for 1, 2 and 4 updates: it had learned to steer by mixing moves.
- Deciding every 0.2 s from the same start: dev courses (top move) 568, 725, 807, 856, 873,
  892 at checks 3.3 minutes apart, then 914, 947, 958, 957, 957, 965 and 971 over the next
  hour, the learning rate falling to 0.00003, and 976 after 30 more minutes on half the
  laptop's processes (the other half ran the restart comparison). Each decision now covers
  four updates, so the laptop gets through 49,000 decisions a second instead of 63,000 but
  four times the flying.
- Its top-move pilot after 20 minutes, flown 4 times on each of 500 courses (only the
  sensors' noise differs): 381 courses landed every time, 22 never.

### What didn't help

- **Practising the courses it failed** (half of its new courses were ones it had just
  failed, kept until it landed them): 806 of 1,000 dev courses after 20 minutes, against 810
  without.
- **A larger learning rate** (three times): 83% of the dev courses against 88% at the same
  point.
- **Restarting practice just before its own failures** (half of its practice flights began
  2 to 6 s before a recent failure of its own, same drone, course and moment): 1,943 of 2,000
  dev courses after 30 minutes, against 1,958 for plain practice run beside it.
- **Practising nearer to the way it is flown.** The final pilot takes its top move but
  practises by drawing moves, and the two part exactly where it dies: its failed flights,
  wound back 2 s, survive 26% of the time by the top move and 73% drawing moves. So I let
  practice sharpen toward the top move (its scores divided by a temperature falling from 1 to
  0.3) for 30 minutes: 1,941 of 2,000 dev courses, against 1,961 for plain practice beside
  it. Plain practice itself had flattened by then: 1,939, 1,958, 1,961 over those two
  half-hours.
- **Second tries,** next.

The final pilot is the plain-practice run of that last comparison. It was chosen on the dev
courses (1,958 of 2,000 taking its top move, 1,919 with moves drawn). The same run 30 minutes
earlier landed 1,939 of them, and 193 test and 195 fresh courses where the final pilot lands
194 and 190: between those two, the test numbers differ by luck. Both were flown once.

### Second tries, in numbers

From the paid-to-land pilot's failed flights, wound back 1, 2 and 3 s, every move on offer
(about 48) was tried as the next move, held 0.3 s, on 8 copies, with the network flying on as
usual. Alive 4 s past the original end: 53 / 65 / 67% drawing the next move as it does, and
78 / 85 / 84% with the best next move (picked on 4 of the copies, scored on the other 4). So
the simulator can say which move would have worked. But fitting the network to 2,300 such
moments from about 770 flights raised its expected survival there from 62% to 79% and on held-out
failed flights from 61.7% to 63.0%, and 2,000 dev courses went from 1,778 landed to 1,750.
The same disease as post 16's copy, with the same cause: too few flights.
