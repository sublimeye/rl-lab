[Post 14](../014-gauntlet/)'s best pilot landed 33 of 200 gauntlet courses. Its closing idea was
to teach a pilot from a scripted one first, then let PPO improve on it. This post does that, in
three steps:
1. **a teacher that can fly the gauntlet.** A planner that, every 0.2 s, copies the simulator
   hundreds of times, flies each copy ahead with a different plan, and follows the best one;
2. **copying it** into post 14's network (two layers of 256, the same inputs) with DAgger;
3. **fine-tuning** the copy with PPO, kept close to the teacher.

The courses, threats, inputs and the 200 test courses are post 14's.

**Short answer:**
- **The teacher works.** Looking further ahead, with sharp dodges and a wait beside the pad,
  the planner lands **200 of 200** test courses (post 14's planner: 150). It still lands 200
  with no foreknowledge of the waypoints and with a bias toward a plain cruise, to be easier to
  copy.
- **The copy doesn't.** Post 14's network fit to it with DAgger lands 1 or 2 of 200 after every
  round. It flies the course but is late on its dodges: 29–39% of single birds hit it.
- **PPO from the copy ends where PPO from scratch does.** Fine-tuned with post 14's school, it
  lands **22 of 200** after 284 minutes; post 14's two seeds reached 33 and 17 in the same
  time. Kept close to the teacher, it didn't improve at all. A bonus for clean dodges didn't
  help (17).

The target was 198 of 200. The best network here lands 22.

## The teacher

A planner sees what no network sees. It copies the whole simulator, so it knows where every bird
in sight will steer and when every frog will strike. That makes it a privileged expert: a
teacher, not a fair opponent for the network.

I started from a planner I'd written at the end of post 14. It holds one of 30 plans (a
cruise height and speed, or holding still) for a second, then the best of 30 more, looking 3.5 s
ahead. It landed 150 of 200 test courses. It was hit twice, by the missile both times, and 48
flights ran out of time:
- 18 hovering beside the pad, under its frog;
- 30 hovering mid-course, 26 of them in front of a waypoint.

**Why it stalled: the horizon.** Its moves were gentle (it climbed and dived at 2.5 m/s at
most), so it could not dodge the missile. Every plan that crossed a missile's waypoint early
ended in a hit inside the 3.5 s look-ahead. A plan that crossed at the very end didn't, because
the missile only arrived after the look-ahead ended. So the best plan was always to cross a
little later, and the drone waited forever. One drone spent 30 s seven metres from the start.

The new planner (`expert.py`) changes four things:
- **Moves.** A plan is up to three moves in a row: cruise at a height and speed; hold the
  current height; a **jink**, both sticks at full deflection for 0.3–0.9 s; land; or wait
  beside the pad. The jinks can start after a delay of up to 3.8 s, so "cruise on, then jink
  in 1.5 s" is a candidate too. Each decision flies 891 candidate plans 4.5 s ahead (1,366
  near the pad).
- **Putting a waypoint off saves nothing.** Every waypoint still ahead costs a fixed amount,
  more for one that releases birds or a missile, and so does a bird or missile still chasing
  the drone when the look-ahead ends. Crossing a waypoint early and dodging what comes out then
  scores better than waiting.
- **Looking further where it matters.** Fixed horizons always have an edge, so the planner
  searches past it in three cases. For a plan that ends with a threat still after it, it tries
  219 dodges for 3 s more. For one that ends near the pad, it tries 132 ways to wait and land.
  For one that crosses a waypoint, it goes back to the moment the threat appears and searches
  dodges from there. Before the last of these, a crossing late in the look-ahead got a deeper
  search than an early one, and the drone sat on its start pillar.
- **Landing under the frog.** The frog strikes at whatever is within 2 m of its column. The
  planner waits 3.2 or 5 m from the pad, just off the pad's height, and lands in a gap between
  strikes.

What each change bought, on the 200 test courses:

| Planner | Landed | Out of time | Hit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Post 14's | 150 | 48 | 2 |
| Jinks, waypoint charges, deeper search at threats and the pad | 192 | 8 | 0 |
| + the hover throttle for the drone's mass and charge | 194 | 6 | 0 |
| + hold-height move, later jinks, more dodge searches | 198 | 2 | 0 |
| + search from the moment a threat appears | **200** | 0 | 0 |

It takes 512 s to fly all 200 courses on the laptop's 10 cores, about a third of a CPU-second
per decision. Its flights take 15.0 s on average; post 14's pilot took 16.6 s on the courses it
landed.

The controllers under the moves cheat a little too. They know the wind and the drone's mass,
and set the tilt and throttle for them. A network has to infer those from its sensors.

## A teacher a network can copy

The first copies were bad: trained on 400 of the planner's flights, the network landed 1 of
200. Two things about the teacher made it hard to copy. The planner could fix both without
landing fewer courses.

**Coin tosses.** When nothing threatens, cruising at 1.5 m and at 11 m score about the same,
and the planner picked whichever came out a hair ahead. A third of its throttle labels were
full up or full down, and they flipped from one update to the next. A network fit to that
learns the average, and the average of "dodge up" and "dodge down" is not dodging. The fix:
among the plans within a **band** of the best score, the planner takes the one whose sticks
right now are closest to a plain cruise (7 m up, 10 m/s). Where the choice doesn't matter, it
always does the same thing; where it must swerve, it swerves least.

To measure how copyable each teacher is, I let it fly 60 training courses, fit a fresh
network to its sticks, and scored the network's sticks against the planner's on 20 other
courses. Squared error, summed over the two sticks:

| Band | Error on other courses | Planner landed (of 80) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (the best plan, always) | 1.17 | 80 |
| 4 | 0.87 | 80 |
| 8 | 0.67 | 80 |
| 16 | **0.53** | 80 |
| 30 | 0.57 | 80 |

With band 16 it still landed 200 of 200 test courses.

**Foreknowledge.** The planner knew where every waypoint was and what each would release. Its
best escapes from the missile started *before* the missile existed: a climb just before the
waypoint, so the missile spawned aimed at the wrong place. Nothing in the network's inputs says a
waypoint is coming. The fix: in the planner's copies, waypoints not yet reached release nothing.
It sees only threats already flying, as the network does. It still landed **200 of 200**, and a
little faster (512 s against 722 s). Its error on other courses: 0.49.

The teacher used from here on has both.

## Copying it: DAgger

Copying the planner only on the planner's own flights (behaviour cloning) teaches nothing about
recovering from the network's own mistakes. DAgger (Ross, Gordon & Bagnell, 2011) adds them:
- **round 0:** the planner flies 400 training courses, and every state the network would have
  seen is stored with the planner's sticks;
- **each round after that:** the network flies 300 courses while the planner, following
  along on the same drones, labels every update with what it would have done. In round *r*
  the planner takes over each update with probability ½<sup>*r*</sup>;
- **the fit:** the network is refit on everything so far.

A full-deflection label only counts against the network if it falls short of full deflection:
the drone clips the sticks anyway.

| Round | Flown by | Its training flights landed | States so far | Test courses landed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | the planner | 400 of 400 | 122,952 | 2 |
| 1 | the network, the planner taking ½ of updates | 56 of 300 | 179,338 | 2 |
| 2 | the network, ¼ | 12 of 300 | 230,705 | 2 |
| 3 | the network, ⅛ | 4 of 300 | 283,039 | 1 |

On the training flights, the network's own share of each flight grows, and the flights land
less. On the test courses the copies land 1 or 2 of 200 in every round. With the earlier,
foreknowing teacher it was 3, 0, 1, 0, 1 over five rounds.

The copies fly the course, but they get hit. On courses with one bird, round 0's copy is hit by
143 of the 492 birds it meets (29%), and round 3's by 163 of 415 (39%). On missile courses,
round 0's copy is hit by 178 of 392 missiles (45%). Post
14's pilot: 46 of 601 birds (8%) and 108 of 504 missiles (21%). On clear courses, with no
threats at all, the copies land none: they never saw one. Round 0's copy goes off the course
or above the ceiling on 99 of them and runs out of time on 90. A typical moment, on one bird course: a bird 1.5 s from passing 0.3 m off. The planner
pushes the throttle up at once and is at full throttle within 0.2 s (0.75, 0.81, 0.87, 0.94,
then 1). Over the same 0.4 s the copy's throttle stays at 0.13 or less (0.04, 0.04, 0.01, 0.03,
0.02, 0.06, 0.11, 0.13). It eases in too late.

Why the copy can't do what the teacher does:
- **The aimed birds hide their commit.** A bird homes in on the drone until about a second
  before contact, then commits to a straight line. Until then, the closest-approach inputs say
  "dead on target" for every aimed bird. The planner dodges at the right moment because it
  knows when the bird will commit. The network sees only that the bird is aimed at it.
- **Exact timing doesn't survive copying.** The planner's dodges are as late and as small as
  will do. A copy that's a little late, or a little short, is hit.
- **Weighting the dangerous moments doesn't help.** Counting states with a threat in sight,
  due within 2 s and within 2 m, ten or thirty times over left the copy hit by 24–28% of single
  birds and 37–41% of missiles. Post 14's pilot was hit by 8% and 21%.
- **Copying the planner ruins a pilot that works.** Starting from post 14's final pilot (33 of
  200) and fitting it to the planner's sticks for 1,000 steps leaves 1 landed; 3,000 steps, 3;
  10,000 steps, 0.

## Fine-tuning with PPO

The plan was to start PPO from the copy and keep it close to the teacher while it learns what
copying couldn't give it. `finetune.py` starts from round 3's copy, with post 14's reward, and
three guards so the first updates don't wreck what was taught:
- **the critic learns alone first:** the network that rates states starts from nothing, so for
  the first 30 updates only it trains;
- **small exploration noise:** the sticks' noise starts at 0.15 or 0.3, not 1;
- **an anchor:** every update pays for the squared distance between its sticks and a frozen
  copy of the taught network, and refits the planner's own sticks on the 283,039 states DAgger
  stored, as DAPG (Rajeswaran et al., 2018) does with demonstrations.

And optionally a **dodge bonus**: 1 point for each aimed bird or missile the drone gets away
from, against 20 for landing.

**Kept close, it stays where it started.** On full test courses, with the anchor, the copy's
sticks stayed within a mean squared distance of about 0.015 of the taught network's. Its training
flights landed under 1%, and it landed 0–3 of the 200 test courses at every checkpoint up to
75M steps (67 min), with or without the dodge bonus. Without the anchor it got worse: 0 of 200
from 30M steps on, a quarter of its training flights running out of time.

**Post 14's school helps, but not the anchor.** Post 14's pilot learned on a mix of drills and
school courses with difficulty dials: each threat starts easy and is turned up as the pilot
copes. Anchored, the copy never turned a dial up in 70M steps (53 min), and landed 0–4 of 200.
A third of its training flights ended lost, off the course or above the ceiling. With the anchor
off (as post 14 trained, but starting from the copy), the dials started turning within 8M steps.

## What I take from it

- **A teacher that wins isn't enough; it has to be one a network can copy.** The planner went
  from 150 to 200 of 200 by searching further, and lost nothing when it gave up foreknowledge
  and coin-toss choices. But it wins with dodges that are as late and as small as will do,
  timed by knowing when each bird commits. A copy that is slightly late is hit. Copying needs a
  teacher that acts on what the student can see, with some margin.
- **A start a little better than random is not a head start.** The copy lands 1 of 200, and PPO
  first pulls it down to 0. After that it learns at post 14's pace and reaches post 14's level.
  What DAgger taught it didn't carry into what PPO learned.
- **Keeping it close to the teacher keeps it where it starts.** With the anchor, every run
  stayed at 0–4 of 200. A copy that crashes isn't worth staying close to.
- **The ceiling is the same.** Post 14's pilots and this one all level off between 17 and 33,
  short on the frogs and on the missile. A better start doesn't move that. The next step is
  probably on the inputs or the network: something that remembers a frog's beat over seconds,
  or sees when a bird commits.
- **198 of 200 would take more than this.** The planner shows the courses can be flown. No
  network here comes close.
